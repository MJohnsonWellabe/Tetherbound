# TETHERBOUND — CANONICAL GAMEPLAY VISION

**Status:** Active owner-directed gameplay vision for Meadows execution.

This document explains what the game is supposed to feel like when all of the existing systems, story, world, and content are assembled correctly. It does not replace `CLAUDE.md`, settled decisions, or `docs/specs/MEADOWS_PROGRESSION_SPEC.md`. Where those files define a hard rule or exact canon detail, they remain authoritative. This document is the experience-level contract that tells Claude/Ralph what all of those pieces are trying to become.

---

# 1. What Tetherbound is

Tetherbound is a third-person open-world creature-training adventure with survival, gathering, crafting, building, exploration, and real-time creature combat.

Its central promise is simple:

> **Build the strongest, most capable, most personally meaningful team of five creatures you can — and live with the choices that five total slots force you to make.**

The player does not build a warehouse of unused creatures. There is no reserve box and no hidden storage loophole. Five creatures means five total.

That rule is not a restriction sitting on top of the game. It is the game’s emotional center.

The player should know their five by name. They should remember where they found them, which fights they survived, which one became a favorite, which one developed a useful trait, which one became their mount, which one evolved, which one needed to be carried through a difficult stretch, and which one they eventually chose to release.

The player is the trainer, explorer, builder, gatherer, and caretaker. **The human never fights.** Creatures fight creatures. When combat begins, the player directly pilots the active creature in real time, aims attacks, moves to avoid danger, builds charged energy, switches creatures, and may physically aim and throw an Orb during wild battles.

The survival/crafting systems exist to support the team journey:

- gathering gives the player materials that unlock useful preparation;
- building creates a home, camps, shelter, beds, crafting and recovery;
- creature beds turn injury into real expedition pressure;
- food and satiety influence readiness without becoming starvation punishment;
- day/night and weather change how the world feels and what can be found;
- better materials and recipes let the player push farther;
- exploration reveals creatures, trainers, resources, TMs, shortcuts, rare encounters and story.

The game is not about surviving for survival’s sake. It is not about building an automated factory. It is not about collecting hundreds of creatures.

Everything should repeatedly feed one question:

> **Is my team of five strong, healthy, prepared, and versatile enough for what is ahead?**

---

# 2. The core loop

The Meadows must repeatedly express this loop:

**Explore → encounter → fight/catch → choose → train → gather → care → prepare → overcome → push farther.**

A good stretch of play should naturally produce several of these decisions:

- I see a creature I might want.
- I see a stronger creature I am not sure I can beat.
- A trainer ahead would be a useful test and source of XP.
- That resource deposit is worth leaving the trail for because I know what it builds.
- One of my creatures is hurt badly enough that continuing has a cost.
- Night is approaching and this clearing would make a good camp.
- I can return home and improve something, or risk pushing on.
- My current five have a weakness I need to solve.
- I have found a sixth creature I genuinely want, so somebody may have to leave.
- There is a major challenge ahead that gives all of this preparation a reason.

Long stretches of merely holding forward are a failure even when the scenery is attractive.

Constant combat is also a failure. The world needs scenic breathing room, discovery, anticipation, and choice. An open field can be quiet while still pulling the player forward through a visible herd, distant trainer, resource outcrop, side trail, structure, ridge, pylon line, weather front, or major landmark.

# 3. What the player should do through the Meadows

The Meadows is the first complete Tetherbound chapter, not a tutorial before the game. A focused first clear should target roughly 3–4 hours, with longer runs for exploration, catching, team experimentation, optional trainers, gathering, building, and side content.

## Opening / home

The player wakes in Grandpa’s farmhouse.

Grandpa is a former trainer who can no longer make the journey himself. The player cannot accidentally skip the required opening. Grandpa provides the starter choice and enough context to establish that Team Tether controls deeper routes and occupies Meadows Hall, and that walking there unprepared is foolish.

The player chooses one of the three unique starters, names it, learns interaction, enters the first real creature fight, and physically catches an early wild creature.

The opening must quickly establish that catching is not the objective by itself. The player is building a team for something.

## First-session goal: become a trainer

The village tournament is the first major proof of readiness.

Before entering it, the player should learn the actual Tetherbound loop by doing it:

- catch creatures for a real team;
- fight with them and gain levels;
- learn switching and team composition;
- gather useful materials;
- unlock basic recipes/build pieces;
- build a small functional home;
- build creature beds;
- build/use the player’s bed;
- experience a creature needing recovery;
- feed/rest the team as required by the shared condition system;
- enter and win the tournament.

This is the game teaching itself through goals, not through a wall of tutorial text.

The opening-to-tournament sequence should feel like a small complete game. If this segment is not fun, later content does not compensate for it.

## Lower Meadows

After the tournament, the player leaves the starting hub with a clearer sense of identity and purpose.

The Lower Meadows should combine broad readable grasslands, farm paths, smaller groves, water features, local trainers, ordinary wild habitats, resources, optional discoveries and at least one special encounter that tempts the player away from the direct route.

The player is building confidence and improving the five for the first physical progression gate at the South Bridge.

The player should not be told “level required.” The world itself creates the gate and the required trainer/story progression opens it.

## Stone & Root / Quarry / Warrens

Beyond the first crossing the chapter becomes materially more demanding.

The Old Quarry and Burrow Warrens introduce Rootstone and the idea that dangerous exploration can unlock new capability.

The player should:

- see and harvest meaningful Rootstone deposits;
- encounter stronger Ground-oriented creatures;
- discover new team-building possibilities;
- clear the required Warrens dungeon;
- face a memorable guardian encounter;
- find useful upgrades/rewards;
- discover evidence of Team Tether activity;
- have at least one optional harder branch or special encounter worth returning for.

The player should emerge feeling that exploring deeply made both the team and the trainer’s preparation materially stronger.

## River / Relay

A substantial river should become an unmistakable regional landmark and a real barrier.

The Old Mill Crossing is unavailable because Team Tether controls the situation and a key person has been captured.

The player moves through a living river region with appropriate wild creatures, trainers, resources, detours and recovery pressure before reaching the Tether Relay.

The Relay is the first significant direct confrontation with Team Tether. Grunts, an officer and Captain Vance form a visible escalation rather than unrelated battles.

Victory frees the captive, disables local machinery, restores the crossing and makes the world physically different. Team Tether has changed from rumor into a threat the player has personally confronted.

## Upper Meadows

Across the river, the player should feel visibly and mechanically stronger than at the beginning.

The Upper Meadows combines broad high pasture, old-growth pockets, ridges, ruins, patrols, Ironwood-tier resources, stronger wild populations, special encounters and the normal riding payoff through Meadowhart and the saddle system.

Three regional captains create the final preparation ladder. Their Sigils physically open the Meadows Hall approach.

The player should be able to look at the team here and feel history:

- creatures gained at different points in the journey;
- levels and moves earned through use;
- injuries and recovery;
- a favorite or Best Creature;
- perhaps an evolved Tuskroot;
- a traversal role that still earns its combat slot;
- at least one creature the player wanted but could not justify keeping.

## Stronghold approach

The final approach must build anticipation rather than become empty connective terrain.

Meadows Hall should grow more visually dominant. Team Tether hardware, pylons, patrols and drained land should become more prominent. Wild encounters should become more dangerous. The player should have one last meaningful opportunity to prepare, rest, adjust the five or pursue a tempting optional challenge before committing to the stronghold.

## Meadows Hall / Warden / legendary

The stronghold is a compact culmination, not a giant puzzle dungeon.

The player advances through the established spaces:

- Outer Works;
- Courtyard / Hall Approach;
- Tether Chamber Approach;
- Warden Arena;
- Legendary Chamber.

Old stone and natural architecture should be visibly occupied and industrialized by Team Tether. Deeper spaces should increasingly reveal that the machinery is drawing power from the captive legendary.

Trainer encounters culminate in the Meadows Warden. This fight should test the team the player spent the whole chapter creating.

After victory, the player disables the tether mechanism and frees the unique Ground legendary.

The legendary voluntarily offers to join.

If the player already owns five creatures, the chapter’s core rule becomes unavoidable: accepting it requires a permanent release choice presented with real ceremony and enough history to hurt.

The final answer of the Meadows should be:

> **These are my five.**

The region then visibly heals and changes. Team Tether barriers and influence recede, rescued people return or acknowledge the result, drained land recovers, the legendary is free, and the world points toward what comes next without entering Biome 2.

# 4. What the Meadows world should look and feel like

The Meadows is one coherent biome with deliberate regional variation.

The macro geography is authored. Major roads, water, caves, bridges, settlements, strongholds, sightlines, region entrances and landmarks are placed intentionally. Wilderness dressing and populations may be rule-driven, but they must serve the authored composition.

The world should alternate between:

- broad rolling grasslands with long sightlines;
- sparse tree lines and copses;
- lush dense pockets near selected ponds, groves, river edges and protected areas;
- rocky quarry/warren terrain;
- river and crossing landmarks;
- elevated pastures and old-growth areas;
- increasingly occupied/drained land near Team Tether infrastructure.

The dense vegetation around the current pond area is an approved lush-area reference. Preserve that quality. Do not extrapolate its density across the whole map.

Open areas must still be authored rather than empty. They gain interest from terrain shape, distant silhouettes, creatures, trainers, resource formations, paths, ruins, weather, camps, pylons and major landmarks.

Each progression band should be a real place with loops, reconnecting paths, overlooks, optional pockets and memorable geography. The world must not read as a single corridor with content placed beside it.

The player should be able to answer without staring at the minimap:

- where did I come from?
- where can I go?
- what region am I in?
- which landmark am I orienting around?
- what looks optional?
- what looks like progress?

# 5. What every system is for

## Combat

Combat is the primary way creatures prove themselves and gain XP. Real-time piloted creature combat should feel readable, responsive and spatial. Major fights need identity, not merely larger HP pools.

## Catching

Catching creates team decisions. The physical throw should be satisfying enough that seeing a desirable wild creature is exciting rather than administrative.

## Wild ecology

Wild creatures are the game’s living world and primary source of team alternatives. Every region should introduce at least one legitimate temptation to change the five.

## Trainers

Trainers are progression tests, sources of XP/rewards, and a human ladder of increasing competence. They should be authored in believable places and escalate from locals to Team Tether hierarchy to the Warden.

## Levels / XP / moves / traits / bond

These systems exist so getting stronger is visible and understandable. The player should always be able to identify several honest ways to improve the team without being forced into repetitive low-value grinding.

## Gathering and crafting

Resources must have known uses. A visible deposit is interesting because the player knows what it can unlock or build, not because every shiny object should be harvested.

## Building

Building supports adventure. A basic shelter should be fast and pleasant to construct. Camps and home should enable recovery, crafting, storage, food and personalization. Building must never become a separate factory game.

## Rest and creature care

Injury creates expedition decisions. A creature placed in a bed physically rests, becomes unavailable, and gradually recovers. Full rest takes meaningful time/overnight. The player should sometimes choose to stop because the team needs it.

## Home

Home should remain emotionally and mechanically relevant throughout the chapter. Returning should provide changing Grandpa dialogue, creature recovery, crafting, progression upgrades, rescued NPC/story acknowledgment and preparation for the next expedition.

## Objectives

The objective system should answer what the player is trying to accomplish now and why the next major challenge matters. It should not become a giant quest engine or a GPS trail.

## Map

The map records exploration and supports the mental map the world already creates. It should not compensate for unreadable geography or become a wild-creature radar.

# 6. The five-creature rule must become real during Meadows

Do not wait until the legendary to demonstrate the five-creature constraint.

By the middle/later chapter, a normal player should have encountered more than five creatures they could reasonably want because of:

- type coverage;
- combat role;
- appraisal/traits;
- attachment;
- evolution potential;
- traversal;
- rarity;
- personal preference.

A successful playtest should produce at least one moment of genuine roster pressure before the legendary.

The final release ceremony only works if the player already understands what it means to give something up.

---

# 7. Progression and reward philosophy

The player should repeatedly know what they are preparing for.

Major challenge ladder:

1. first wild encounter;
2. village trainers / tournament;
3. South Bridge progression;
4. Warrens guardian;
5. Tether Relay / Captain Vance;
6. Upper Meadows regional captains;
7. stronghold trainers / elite;
8. Meadows Warden.

Levels are not UI locks. Expected team strength should emerge naturally from the available encounters, XP, rewards, catches and resources.

Every detour should pay into preparation through one or more of:

- XP;
- bond;
- a desirable catch opportunity;
- useful materials;
- a TM;
- a recipe/build unlock;
- a consumable;
- coins with a meaningful use;
- a shortcut;
- world information;
- a rare trait/special individual;
- emotional/world discovery.

Avoid empty rewards and avoid grind whose only purpose is increasing a number.

# 8. Definition of a finished region

A region is not finished because its data exists or its triggers fire.

A finished region must have:

- recognizable geography;
- a clear reason to enter;
- ordinary wild ecology;
- at least one meaningful team-building temptation;
- useful gathering/resource opportunities;
- trainer/challenge presence appropriate to the region;
- at least one optional discovery or detour;
- at least one memorable encounter;
- a sensible camp/recovery opportunity where the journey warrants one;
- visible navigation and route hierarchy;
- a clear transition/payoff into the next region;
- no long purposeless travel stretch;
- day/night readability;
- acceptable target-hardware performance.

It must also pass a continuous playthrough from the prior gate to the next gate.

# 9. How Ralph/Claude should build this

Do not treat the repository as a flat checklist of independent features.

The active execution order is defined in `docs/ROADMAP.md`.

Existing backlog items remain real and must not be discarded. They are grouped under gameplay gates and regional packages so their implementation contributes to a finished experience.

When a current implementation is already correct, verify and preserve it rather than rewriting it.

When an approach repeatedly fails the player-facing quality bar, stop micro-tuning and rebuild the solution while preserving settled design, data and good infrastructure.

For each gameplay gate:

1. inspect current `main`;
2. implement/fix the child systems required for that segment;
3. play the whole segment continuously using the real player path;
4. record what the player is trying to do, what team progression occurred, what choices appeared and where travel felt dead;
5. fix the segment until it meets the gate criteria;
6. continue automatically to the next gate.

These are not owner-blocking approval gates. Do not stop and wait for the owner unless a genuinely unresolved design choice requires it.

# 10. Ultimate Meadows acceptance

A fresh player should be able to play from waking in Grandpa’s house through defeating the Warden and freeing the legendary and say, through their actions rather than a survey:

- I understood what I was trying to become strong enough to do.
- I cared which creatures made my five.
- I repeatedly found reasons to fight, catch, explore, gather and prepare.
- Building and rest mattered because they supported the journey.
- Different parts of the Meadows felt like distinct real places.
- I was tempted off the direct route.
- I did not spend long periods merely running through empty scenery.
- My team at the end felt earned and different from my team at the beginning.
- The Warden felt like the culmination of the chapter.
- Freeing the legendary and choosing the final five mattered.
- The world looked changed because of what I did.

The Meadows is done when it feels like a complete enjoyable game chapter, not when every system exists separately.
