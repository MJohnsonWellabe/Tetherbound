# Visual Judge Report — after-fix pass (18 frames)

Judged blind against `docs/reference/tetherbound-meadows-keyart.png` (key art board) and
`docs/reference/palworld-0*.jpg` (bar reference), per `.claude/skills/visual-judge/SKILL.md`.
No prior report or config was consulted; this is a fresh read of the frames as delivered.

---

## shots/ (framed survey, 5 frames)

### 01-spawn-outward.png
- Eye lands: (1) the two rounded grey boulders on the near rise, dead centre — they are the
  darkest, largest, most contrasted shapes in the frame; (2) the player character standing
  just in front of them, then the elder NPC to the right by the garden beds.
- Asking the viewer to look at: the immediate dooryard/garden area the player is about to
  walk into — a "here's your starting task" establishing shot.
- Defects: grass card billboards in the deep-shadow foreground read as flat black cutouts
  with no volume — the giant shadow being cast across the whole lawn (from an off-screen
  object) is a large flat rectangle-with-notch shape that does not match anything visible
  in frame, which reads as a lighting/shadow-caster bug rather than a real object's shadow.
  The dead tree at left (bare grey branches) is a jarring, unhealthy silhouette to place at
  the game's own front door. Player character's silhouette is legible; the elder NPC's is
  slightly muddy against the fence behind him (similar mid-tones).

### 02-valley-floor.png
- Eye lands: (1) the single huge dark boulder centre-frame, easily the largest and darkest
  mass in the picture; (2) the small distant cottage cluster at left-of-centre on the
  horizon, which the eye finds second because it's the only "story" element (roofs, warm
  red-orange accent) against otherwise uniform green.
- Asking the viewer to look at: scale/vista — a big rock landmark with a village glimpsed
  beyond it, meant to sell "rolling hills, distant settlement."
- Defects: the extreme foreground is filled with repeated identical flower/bush props at
  regular, obviously-scattered intervals (bottom-right especially) — reads as procedural
  placement, not authored planting. The boulder's near face is nearly flat black with no
  gradation, so it reads as a silhouette cutout rather than a lit 3D rock. Player is a tiny,
  almost illegible smudge left-of-centre — no rim light or contrast separating them from the
  grass behind them.

### 03-rise-overlook.png
- Eye lands: (1) the small village cluster (white walls, red-orange roofs) sitting in the
  green bowl below — the only warm, detailed, small-silhouette object against a huge flat
  green/khaki field, so it wins immediately; (2) the cluster of rocks on the near ridge
  (one reddish-brown, two grey) bottom-right of centre.
- Asking the viewer to look at: the overlook vista — "here is the whole valley and your
  destination village," a wayfinding/scale shot.
- Defects: this is the frame most damaging to the horizon/depth criterion in the set — the
  ground plane beyond roughly 40% depth turns into a flat, textureless green-grey wash with
  a hard, visible horizon line and no atmospheric gradation, aerial perspective, or
  micro-detail (no trees, no rolling shading) all the way to the sky. It reads as a
  low-res heightmap texture rather than terrain. The village itself is a good, readable
  small landmark, but it is floating in a featureless void. Foreground rocks are strong
  and well-lit by contrast, which only makes the emptiness behind them more obvious.

### 04-three-quarter.png
- Eye lands: (1) the timber-and-stone cottage with the dark red roof, dead centre on the
  horizon — clearly the intended focal landmark, warm roof colour against cool sky; (2) the
  flanking dark conifer/round trees either side of the cottage that frame it.
- Asking the viewer to look at: the cottage as a wayfinding landmark at the end of an open
  field — closest frame in the set to the key art's "starting settlement" panel.
  Defects: the entire foreground two-thirds of the frame is a single unbroken lawn of
  identical small white flowers at even, machine-like spacing — no clearings, no clumping,
  no variation in density, which is the single most "generator output" foreground in the
  survey. The little dark box/shape at bottom-left edge is unreadable — could be a rock, a
  bag, or a piece of geometry; it isn't legible as anything.

### 05-spawn-low-sun.png
- Eye lands: (1) the moon/pale sun disk high in the sky, upper-right — brightest point in
  the frame; (2) the two boulders and player figure below it, same composition as frame 01.
- Asking the viewer to look at: the same dooryard composition as 01 but at a different time
  of day, presumably to sell day/night or golden-hour mood.
- Defects: this frame doesn't convincingly read as "low sun" — the sky is a warm-grey overcast
  wash rather than a warm golden-hour gradient, and the ground/grass colour is barely
  distinguishable from the midday frame (01) apart from being slightly darker. The key art's
  night/dusk panel shows a clear warm-to-purple sky gradient and rim-lit character; this frame
  has neither. Same oversized, geometry-mismatched ground shadow defect as frame 01, now even
  harder to read against the duller lighting.

## shots_places/ (5 frames)

### place1-gate-meadow.png
- Eye lands: (1) the tall, well-lit central tree with the sunlit canopy, right-of-centre —
  brightest, tallest, most detailed silhouette; (2) the dirt path leading up through the
  meadow toward the treeline gap between the tree cluster left and the lone tree right.
- Asking the viewer to look at: an entry/gate into a grove — the path is clearly meant to
  draw the eye up and through the trees.
- Defects: the flower/bush understory is planted in near-perfect diagonal rows (very visible
  running from lower-left to upper-right) — one of the clearest "grid/scatter" tells in the
  set. A pale horse/creature silhouette is visible on the horizon between the trees but is
  small, flat, and easy to miss — if it's meant to be a wildlife beat it doesn't land in a
  still. Rock and tree trunks are well-differentiated in silhouette from each other, which is
  a genuine strength of this frame.

### place2-the-rise.png
- Eye lands: (1) the huge, near-black tree trunk that fills the top-centre of frame and
  cuts off — it dominates by sheer size and darkness; (2) the smaller trees strung along the
  horizon left-to-right, read second as a loose treeline.
- Asking the viewer to look at: unclear — the giant trunk is cropped by the top of frame and
  doesn't resolve into a canopy or a clear subject, so the frame doesn't finish its own
  sentence. The dirt patch bottom-right competes for attention as the second-brightest area.
- Defects: the oversized cropped trunk reads as a framing/camera-placement mistake — it's
  large enough and close enough that the shot appears to have been taken while clipped
  into or immediately beside the tree, not composed around it. No clear horizon landmark
  (no village, no water, no rock formation) to anchor the "rise" concept the name implies.

### place3-pond-pocket.png — special check (three-element test)
Asked to check for a bench/campfire seating area, a body of water, and a windmill/mill on
the far shore, together in one frame.
- **Body of water: identifiable, clearly.** A large, calm, light-teal pond fills the left
  half to centre of the frame with a soft shoreline and visible ripple texture — the
  strongest, most legible water read in the whole survey.
- **Campfire/seating: partially identifiable.** There is a lit campfire (a glowing orange
  light source with a ring of grey stones) at the player's feet in the near foreground,
  right of centre. It is **not occluded** — it's in open ground and clearly lit. However,
  there is no distinct bench or seat object visible near it; what's present reads as
  "campfire" alone, not "campfire + seating." If a bench prop was intended here, it is not
  present/legible in this frame, not hidden behind anything.
- **Windmill/mill on far shore: not clearly identifiable as a windmill.** Two buildings sit
  on the far shore / a small island in the pond — a tall, narrow, half-timbered building
  with a dark-red roof (mill/house-shaped, multi-storey) and a smaller single-storey
  building beside it with a matching red roof. Neither building shows visible sails, blades,
  or any other windmill-specific silhouette element; they read as generic village
  cottage/tower shapes. This is not an occlusion problem (nothing blocks the view — both
  structures are in clean silhouette against open sky and water) — the windmill identity
  simply isn't legible from the geometry/silhouette present. If it is meant to be a
  windmill, it needs a recognisable feature (sail blades, mill wheel) to read as one; as
  built it reads as "lakeside house," not "mill."
- General notes: this is one of the strongest frames in the set for depth and landmark
  legibility — pond, far-shore structures, and mid-ground trees all separate cleanly.
  Foreground reed/grass billboards on the right are a little flat and repeat at obvious
  intervals but do not hurt readability at speed.

### place4-long-field.png
- Eye lands: (1) the player character, dead centre, silhouetted against a bright gap in the
  treeline; (2) the dark, boxy shape (roof/hut?) glimpsed through the trees at right-of-
  centre, which is hard to identify.
- Asking the viewer to look at: the player about to enter a treeline/grove ahead — a

- Defects: the dark object at right (possibly a rooftop or rock) is under-lit to the point

### place5-bridge-approach.png
- Eye lands: (1) the sunlit dirt path curving from foreground into the distance, the single
  brightest, largest continuous shape in the frame; (2) the line of trees on the right
  horizon where the path seems to lead.
- Asking the viewer to look at: a route/traversal beat — "the path continues this way,"
  reinforced by a wooden bridge rail just visible mid-frame right.
- Defects: no bridge structure is actually legible in this frame despite the filename — at
  most there's a low wooden rail/plank hinted at centre-right, but it does not read as a
  bridge crossing; if this is meant to establish "you are approaching a bridge" it does not
  land. Left-foreground canopy is very dark and slightly clips into frame, reading as
  camera-too-close-to-foliage rather than an intentional framing element.

- Eye lands: (1) the gap between the two large flanking trunks, through which a distant

## Cross-frame patterns worth naming once

- **Cropped-canopy trunks recur across five frames** (place2, comp1, comp3, comp4 x2) —
  a large trunk fills the near field and is cut off by the top of frame with no canopy
  resolving. Whether or not this is the camera-geometry bug this pass targeted, it is still
  visibly present and still reads as a placement/height problem in these particular frames.
- **"Bridge" and "pond" in four filenames (place5, comp3, comp6, comp7, comp8) show no
  bridge and, in three of five, no pond** — either the shot locations don't match their
  names, or the features referenced aren't currently placed/visible from these camera
  positions.
- **Foreground flora is planted at visibly regular intervals** in the majority of frames
  (01, 02, 04, place1, place4, comp1-8 to varying degrees) — this is the most consistent,
  repeatable "generator output" tell across the whole survey.
- **Terrain beyond the near/mid field routinely goes flat and textureless** (worst in
  03-rise-overlook, also visible in 02-valley-floor) — a hard, low-detail horizon rather
  than graduated distance.

## The three things that most separate these frames from the references

1. **Empty, textureless distance vs. the key art's and Palworld's continuously detailed
   ground plane.** In `03-rise-overlook.png` and `02-valley-floor.png`, everything past
   the first third of the frame collapses into flat green-khaki wash with a hard horizon
   line and no rolling shading, no distant tree cover, no aerial perspective. The key art's
   "Rolling Hills" panel and every Palworld reference (especially palworld-02 and
   palworld-04) keep visible ground texture, tree cover and value variation all the way to
   the horizon. This is the single largest gap and it is fixable in-scene: more distant
   tree/prop scatter, terrain shading detail, and fog/aerial-perspective tuning would close
   most of it without new art.

2. **Grid-regular foreground planting vs. authored clustering.** Nearly every frame with
   foreground flowers/bushes (04-three-quarter, place1-gate-meadow, comp1 through comp8)
   shows them at conspicuously even spacing and uniform scale — visible as diagonal or grid
   rows at a glance. The key art and Palworld references show grass/flowers in irregular
   clumps with bare patches between them. This is also fixable in-scene: randomising
   scatter density, adding clearings, and varying prop scale would address it directly.

3. **No creature or character presence to judge, and the trainer reads flat/small against
   its own world.** Every one of these 18 frames is an empty landscape or a lone player
   back-silhouette — there is no creature in any frame, and the reference bar (Palworld,
   and the key art's "Day/Night" panel) is explicitly built around a person-plus-creature
   pair reading together at human scale. Without any creature present, the game's own
   namesake subject cannot be judged at all here, and the human figure alone doesn't carry
   comparable interest — in frames like 02-valley-floor and place2-the-rise the player is a
   small, low-contrast smudge easy to lose against the grass. This gap is not fixable by
   scene dressing alone — it requires shots that actually include a creature in frame,
   and/or better contrast/rim-lighting on the player silhouette.

## The two bar questions

**A. Do these frames read as belonging to the world in the key art board?**
**No — partial.** Palette and prop language (oak-ish rounded trees, wooden fences, warm
cottage roofs, wildflower ground cover) are recognisably the same family as the key art's
"Starting Settlement" and "Rolling Hills" panels, and a few frames (place3-pond-pocket,
comp8-bridge-rim, comp4-rise-look-back) get close to that board's mood. But the key art's
defining qualities — continuous, richly-detailed ground cover reaching to the horizon,
graduated atmospheric depth, and warm global illumination — are absent from the weaker
frames (03-rise-overlook, 02-valley-floor), which instead show flat, textureless distance.
The set is inconsistent: some frames belong to this world, several do not yet.

**B. Shown beside the Palworld references, would someone say these are trying to be the
same kind of game?**
**No.** The stylised-cutout rendering (flat-shaded, low-poly foliage cards, hard-edged
trunks) sits well short of Palworld's density, saturation and lit-surface detail, and
critically **no frame contains a creature** — Palworld's screenshots are built around
creature-and-character pairs at legible scale, which is the entire point of comparison.
An empty field, however nicely lit, does not read as "trying to be the same kind of game"
as art whose subject is always a creature doing something. Ground/foliage density is also
markedly lower than every Palworld reference; even Palworld's quietest frame
(palworld-05-base-building) has continuous, textured ground cover and background detail
that these frames only achieve in their best moments (place3, comp8).

**What's fixable by changing the scene:** ground/foliage density and irregular scatter,
distant terrain detail and fog/depth tuning, canopy-crop camera framing on the five
affected shots, and giving the player figure more contrast against the grass. These do not
require new art.

**What is not fixable by scene changes alone:** the complete absence of any creature in
this 18-frame set, which is the largest single factor in the "no" to question B — the
comparison the owner set cannot be judged fairly without a creature in frame at all, and
no amount of foliage density fixes that. If existing creature assets already exist
elsewhere in the project, a future survey pass should include at least one frame with a
creature and the 1.80 m trainer together, per the rubric's own scale-agreement criterion,
which none of these 18 frames currently allow this critic to check at all.
