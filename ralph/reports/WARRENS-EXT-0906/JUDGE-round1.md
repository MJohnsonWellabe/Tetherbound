# Visual judgment — Burrow Warrens (after)

Frames: 01–02 (knoll from outside), 03 (mouth), 04 (hall dressing), 05 (hall from
the doorway), 06 (den and guardian), 07 (den dressing), plus the contact sheet.
References: `tetherbound-meadows-keyart.png`, `palworld-0*.jpg`.

## Exterior first — does this read as a burrow warren dug into a bank? (01–03)

**No, not yet, and the read fails for a specific reason: there is no bank.** A
"burrow warren" promises a mound or hillside that large diggers have hollowed
out — a landform with a wound in it. What frames 01 and 02 show instead is flat
to gently-rolling open meadow (grass, two accent trees — one normal green, one
solid flat red — a few boulders) with no rise, ridge or mound anywhere near the
implied entrance. In frame 02 there is a low earthen hump behind the red tree
that could be the top of a burrow, but it reads as background scenery, not as
the thing the player is walking toward — it's smaller than the tree beside it
and sits at the same height as the ground clutter. There is no landmark
silhouette here at all: cover 01 or 02 at 30% and nothing says "dungeon
entrance," only "meadow." Compare the keyart's stronghold or standing-stone
panels, or Palworld's plateau-landmark shot (`palworld-04`) — both spend the
whole frame building toward a single unmistakable shape on the skyline. These
two frames spend the whole frame on grass.

Frame 03 is the actual mouth, and once you're pointed at it the shape reads
correctly in the broadest sense — a dark arched hole punched into an earthen
face, flanked by rock, capped with dead branches and roots hanging into the
opening, with a red tree beside it for a colour landmark. That's the right
idea. But the execution has real defects:

- **The bank has no depth or geology.** The earth face around the arch is a
  single flat brown slab with a hard, straight top edge — it reads as a
  rectangular block of clay with a hole cut in it, not as a hillside worn into
  a shape by a burrowing animal. There's no slope leading up over it, no
  visible mass of the mound rising above and behind the opening, no rounded
  "buried dome" silhouette the way a real warren mound would read. It looks
  like a concrete culvert entrance, not a dug den.
- **Flat, textureless shading.** The dirt face, the boulders either side, and
  the path leading up to the mouth are all nearly the same uniform mid-brown
  value with no visible surface detail (no root tangles, no claw-gouges, no
  loose spoil-earth mounded at the entrance the way a real digger would leave
  tailings). The only high-detail element in the whole shot is the dead-branch
  crown perched on top, which sits on the slab like a hat rather than growing
  out of it.
- **Colour focus fights the subject.** The saturated red tree at frame-right is
  the single most eye-catching object in 01, 02 and 03 — the eye goes to it
  first every time, not to the entrance. A warren mouth needs to be the
  loudest shape in the frame; here it's competing with, and often losing to,
  a tree that has nothing to do with the location.
- **Scale is ambiguous but plausibly wrong.** The arch is maybe 2.5–3x the
  height of the visible interior character silhouette glimpsed through the
  door (see below), which is in the right range for an entrance sized for a
  "large digging creature," but because there's no human or vehicle in the
  exterior shot for a hard reference, and because the earth mass around the
  arch is so flat and shallow, the opening reads more like a doorway punched
  into a wall than a burrow scaled to something bigger than a person.
- **Grass and scatter (01, 02) are generic meadow, not warren approach.** The
  grass card style, boulder placement and single-species scatter are the same
  language as an open Meadows field elsewhere in the game — nothing in the
  approach signals "creatures dug here": no spoil piles, no claw marks, no
  thinning canopy, no worn track converging on a hole. The only sign this is a
  dungeon approach is the paved-looking grey path picked up faintly in frame
  03's foreground steps.

**Verdict on the exterior:** this currently reads as *meadow with a hole in a
wall*, not *warren mound*. The single biggest fix is giving the earth around
the mouth actual bulk and a rounded, rising silhouette so it can be recognised
before the player is looking straight at the arch, and pulling the red tree's
saturation down (or moving it) so the entrance, not the tree, owns the frame.

## Interior frames (04–07), briefer

- **04 (hall dressing):** Serviceable but very dark and flat — the rock wall
  texture on the left is one of the few well-detailed surfaces in the whole
  set, but the crate/barrel prop cluster sits in near-total blackness at
  screen centre-right, and the ceiling beam crossing the top of frame is an
  unlit grey slab with no shadow catching it. The scene reads as "dark room
  with props," not as a lived-in burrow interior. A silhouette-shaped spider
  is barely visible on the right wall — nice mystery touch if visible at all,
  invisible at a glance here.
- **05 (hall from the doorway):** The best-composed interior frame — the giant
  glowing mushroom cluster in the foreground gives real scale contrast and a
  distinct silhouette, and the framed doorway in the middle distance creates
  depth. But it exposes the scale problem directly: through that doorway a
  creature (the guardian, matching 06) and a second black-and-white badger-
  type creature are both visible crouched at roughly a **third of the doorway
  height** — for a "large digging creature" den, a doorway sized for these
  animals should be filled by them, not dwarfed by them.
- **06 (den and guardian):** This is where the scale defect is unambiguous.
  The guardian is described as a large armoured digger meant to read as a
  boss/threat, but measured against the doorway on the left of frame (a
  standard person-height opening) the creature's shoulder height is well under
  half the doorway's height — it looks knee-high to waist-high on a human,
  the size of a large dog, not something that reads as bigger and more
  dangerous than the 1.80 m trainer. Its pose (head-down, hunched, facing
  away/down) doesn't help — a guardian should present a threatening silhouette
  toward the entrance it's guarding, not turn its back to the room. The prop
  reads decently close-up (rock-plated shell, some texture variation) but at
  the size and pose shown it will not land as an intimidating "boss" the way
  frame 06's slot in the sequence implies.
- **07 (den dressing):** Similar issue to 04 — underlit, flat pillars/wall
  strips read as regular vertical bands (mildly regular/procedural), a loot
  sack and crate sit in a pool of the only real light in frame. The pale
  mushroom cluster reappears at right, which is a good recurring landmark
  prop for this location, but the rest of the frame is empty sandy floor with
  little incident.

## The three biggest gaps from the references, ranked

1. **No mound, no bank, no landmark silhouette (frames 01–03).** Both
   references stake everything on the promise that you can identify a distant
   destination by shape alone — the keyart's ruined stronghold and standing
   stone, Palworld's jagged plateau tower (`palworld-04`). These frames have no
   equivalent rising landform at all; the "warren" is a flat wall with a hole,
   discoverable only once the camera is already pointed at it. This is fixable
   with scene work: sculpt actual bank/mound geometry rising over and around
   the arch, add a converging worn path and spoil-earth at the base.
2. **Guardian creature reads roughly a third to a half the size it needs to be
   (frames 05, 06).** The rubric's own worked example — a supposedly
   large creature rendering smaller than expected — is happening here plains
   as day: measured against the doorway and the implied 1.80 m trainer, this
   "large armoured digger" is dog-sized, not room-filling. This is fixable
   without new art if the mesh is simply the wrong instance scale; it becomes
   an art problem only if the base mesh has no larger variant to scale up to.
3. **Flat, undifferentiated lighting and surfacing everywhere except the rock
   wall texture in 04.** Palworld's field-boss shot (`palworld-03`) and the
   keyart both show real light falling across form — highlights, cast
   shadows, colour variation across a single surface. Here the earth bank,
   interior walls (aside from the one good rock texture), floor and ceiling
   beams are close to single flat mid-tones with weak or absent shadow
   contact, so nothing in the space has been "lit," only illuminated evenly.
   This is fixable in-scene (light placement, contact shadows, texture
   variation) without new assets.

## Bar questions

**A. Do these frames read as belonging to the world in
`tetherbound-meadows-keyart.png`?** **No.** The grass/foliage/sky treatment in
01–02 is consistent with the Meadows palette (green grass, blue sky, warm
accent foliage), so the *biome* is recognisable. But the keyart's entire
visual grammar is built on landmark silhouettes read from a distance — a ruin
on a hill, a standing stone against a sunset, a mountain behind a village —
and that grammar is completely absent from the exterior shots here. A flat
earth wall with an arch cut in it does not carry the "landmark visible from
distance" quality the art direction notes name explicitly.

**B. Shown beside `palworld-0*.jpg`, would someone say these are trying to be
the same kind of game?** **No.** Palworld's frames are built around dense,
detailed ground cover, strong value contrast, and creatures whose scale and
presence dominate their surroundings (the pink creature in `palworld-03`
fills a third of the frame and dwarfs the player). These frames have thin,
uniform-height grass scatter, largely flat mid-tone lighting, and a marquee
creature (the guardian) that is smaller than the doorway it stands in. The
gap is mostly in density/scale/lighting execution rather than palette, which
argues most of it is fixable in-scene: thicken and vary the ground scatter,
rebuild the bank as an actual landform, rescale the guardian, and add real
directional light with contact shadows to the interior. The one piece that
may not be scene-fixable is whether the current guardian mesh has enough
detail/silhouette interest to read as a boss at the correct larger scale —
that needs a check once it's resized, and may turn into an asset question if
the answer is no.
