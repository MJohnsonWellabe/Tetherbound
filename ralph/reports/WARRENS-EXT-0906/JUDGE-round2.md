# Visual judgment — Burrow Warrens (round 2)

Frames judged: `_sheet_warrens_round2.png` and the seven individual frames
(01–07). References used: `docs/reference/tetherbound-meadows-keyart.png`
and `docs/reference/palworld-0*.jpg`. Compatibility/software-GL render —
judged for composition, silhouette, colour relationship and scale, not fine
lighting.

## Exterior (01–03) — judged first and hardest

**The bank does not read as a landmark mound.** Across all three exterior
frames the earth bank is a smooth, uniformly curved ridge wrapped in a
regular diagonal ribbing pattern that repeats at a fixed interval over the
whole face (clearest in 02, continues onto the mound above the entrance in
03). That regularity is the tell: it reads as a tiled/striated material on a
simple displaced shape — a sand dune or a canvas tent — not an eroded,
clawed-open earth bank. There is no rock outcrop breaking the slope, no
exposed root mass, no rubble or spoil pile, no patchy grass-on-slope; the
whole bank is one flat khaki-brown value from base to crest. Compare the
naturalistic, materially-varied terrain forms in the key art (moss-topped
rock, exposed strata, standing stone) or Palworld's plateau landmark (ref
04) — both read as places that were *shaped by* something, not generated as
a primitive. This is the single biggest gap between these frames and both
references.

**Frame 01 hides the landmark behind foreground trees.** The two large,
glossy-dark-green trees at right occupy the strongest part of the frame and
the bank itself is reduced to a thin sliver of tan crest at top-mid,
mostly cropped by the frame edge. A player walking toward this would not
identify a mound-with-a-burrow silhouette from here — the framing defeats
the "landmark visible from distance" goal before the mound even gets a
chance to read.

**Frame 02 gives the bank a clear profile but the profile is wrong.** This
is the widest, cleanest look at the mound and it confirms the ribbed/tent
read described above. Two small dark smudges near the base could be
secondary den holes but are illegible at this distance — they read as mud
stains, not entrances. The dead gray tree and the single green tree
planted on the crest are too small and undifferentiated to sell scale or
add landmark silhouette variety; they look like set-dressing stuck on top
of a primitive rather than vegetation that grew there.

**Frame 03's entrance itself is closer to working, but is too clean and
too crowded by colour.** The dark rounded arch reads as a tunnel mouth, and
the pair of dead sticks/roots snagged over the top is a good touch that
suggests something forced its way through. But the arch is a
perfectly regular, symmetric cut with a hard edge — architecturally clean
rather than clawed-open and ragged — and it repeats as an identical
cross-section receding down the tunnel, reinforcing a "carved corridor"
read over "dug burrow." The pale flagstone-textured threshold in front of
the mouth looks like a paved patio slab, not packed dirt with paw-prints or
a spoil mound — the wrong material for what should be the messiest, most
disturbed ground in the shot.

**Ground and colour, all three frames.** The ground plane is a pale,
washed-out grayish-green — noticeably desaturated next to the rich greens
of the key art meadow and every Palworld reference. Layered on top of that
pale ground, a single tree with pure, highly saturated red foliage sits at
frame-right in 01 and 03 and is the most visually dominant object in both
compositions by contrast alone — it pulls focus away from the mound in 01
and away from the entrance (the actual subject) in 03. One saturated red
accent competing with the intended focal point in the same frame as that
focal point is a colour-focus defect, not a stylistic choice — nothing else
in the frame earns that much visual weight.

## Interior (04–07) — briefer pass

The corridor and den read as reasonably spacious and are lit with
warm point lights that carry some mood (05's doorway glow, 06's spotlit
guardian). Some specific issues:

- **04**: the rock-pillar material at left is a bright, sparkly
  granite-countertop texture — an odd choice for a clawed-out dirt burrow,
  and it reads as "carved stone" rather than "dug earth." The background is
  crushed near-black with a floating black spider-leg silhouette on the
  ceiling beam that's ambiguous (web decal? overhead prop?) and a
  saturated teal-blue rock at right that doesn't belong in this palette at
  all — it's the only cool-hued object in either the exterior or interior
  set.
- **05**: the mushroom cluster blocking the corridor is made of near-
  identical caps at near-identical height and rotation, tightly packed —
  reads as a repeated prop stamped down rather than a grown cluster
  (rubric's "intentionality" flag). The same sparkly granite wall material
  from 04 continues here, still mismatched with the wooden support beams.
- **06 — guardian scale.** This is the important one: the guardian does
  not read as a threat. Using the doorway opening at left and the crate at
  right as rulers, the creature's shoulder height is roughly a third of the
  doorway height and only modestly taller than the crate — well under the
  1.80 m trainer, let alone big enough to feel like the boss of a dungeon.
  Compare the Palworld boss frames (01, 03 in the reference set), where the
  field boss visibly dwarfs the player and everything else in frame. Here
  the "large armoured digger" premise is undercut by the render: it looks
  like a mid-size burrow critter, not a guardian. The bright, clean green
  plant beside it is also oddly vivid and manicured for underground
  flora — it reads as a potted houseplant, not cave growth.
- **07**: reasonable small dressing (sacks, chest, cone-shaped rock), but
  the same floating dark spider silhouette reappears top-left, and the
  upper-left corner crushes to near-black, losing the dressing that's
  presumably there.

A secondary pattern worth naming: the exterior mound (01–03) and the small
rock prop in the den (06/07) share the same smooth, ribbed cone silhouette.
If that's the same rock asset reused at two scales, it explains why the
landmark itself doesn't look purpose-built — it's the same generic prop the
scene uses everywhere else for "rock," just scaled up.

## Verdict

### The three things that most separate these frames from the references

1. **The landmark bank is a tiled-material primitive, not an eroded earth
   form.** The key art's terrain (moss rock, exposed strata, standing
   stones) and Palworld's plateau landmark (ref 04) are shaped, varied
   silhouettes; this bank is one smooth ridge with a repeating diagonal
   stripe texture and no rock, root or rubble breaking it (frames 01, 02,
   03).
2. **The guardian does not read as a threat.** Palworld's boss frames (refs
   01, 03) make the boss the largest, most dominant shape in the
   composition, towering over the player. In frame 06 the guardian is
   barely taller than a loot crate and about a third of the doorway height
   beside it — smaller than the trainer ruler this project uses, when the
   role calls for the opposite.
3. **Desaturated ground plus one competing saturated colour, not a
   cohesive palette.** The key art and every Palworld reference commit to
   rich, saturated natural greens with colour reserved for real focal
   points. Here the ground is pale gray-green (01–03) while a single
   pure-red tree pulls all the saturation to itself and away from the
   mound/entrance that should be the subject (01, 03).

### The two bar questions

**A. Do these frames read as belonging to the world in the Tetherbound key
art?** **No.** The key art's terrain reads as sculpted and materially rich
even in long shot; this bank reads as a tiled primitive shape, the ground
is paler than anything in the key art, and the one strong colour accent
(the red tree) is not a hue the key art palette uses anywhere near this
saturation.

**B. Shown beside the Palworld references, would someone say these are
trying to be the same kind of game?** **No.** The interior dressing and
lighting are in the right neighbourhood, but the exterior landmark reads
as a placeholder shape rather than an authored one, and the guardian —
the one creature these frames actually show — reads too small to be a
dungeon boss, which is the opposite of how Palworld stages its bosses.

### What's fixable by changing the scene vs. what needs new art

**Fixable in the scene (no new art required):**
- Guardian scale — this is a scale/transform value; make the creature
  bigger relative to the doorway and crate so it reads as the room's
  largest object.
- Ground and red-tree saturation — recolour/re-tint the ground material
  toward the richer green seen in the key art; either desaturate the red
  tree toward an autumn rust that doesn't out-compete the entrance, move it
  out of frame-dominant position, or use it more sparingly.
- Frame 01 camera/tree placement — pull the foreground trees back or
  reposition the survey camera so the mound silhouette isn't cropped and
  blocked.
- Cave wall material — swap the sparkly granite-countertop texture for an
  earthen/dirt material consistent with a dug burrow; recolour the one
  teal-blue rock to an earth tone.
- Mushroom cluster — vary cap size/rotation/spacing so it reads as grown
  rather than stamped.
- Entrance threshold — replace or re-texture the pale flagstone path with
  packed dirt, mud, or a spoil pile so the entrance reads as freshly dug
  rather than paved.

**Likely needs new/modified art, not just scene tuning:**
- The bank's silhouette and surface itself — if the ribbed stripe pattern
  is baked into the mound's geometry/normal map rather than a re-tileable
  material, giving it an organic, rock-and-root-broken silhouette is a
  reshaping or re-texturing job on that asset, not a placement fix.
- If the recurring cone-shaped rock (exterior mound and den prop) is in
  fact one shared prop reused at multiple scales, breaking that repetition
  needs an additional rock silhouette, not just more copies of the same
  one.
