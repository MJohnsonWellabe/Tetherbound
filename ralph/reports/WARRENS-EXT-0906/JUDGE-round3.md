# Burrow Warrens — Visual Judge, Round 3

## Exterior (01–03): does the bank read as a landmark, does the mouth read as a burrow?

**01-knoll-from-outside.** The "bank" is a single smooth, uniformly dark-olive hill mesh with two blocky brown-and-tan rock outcrops stuck on top like they were dropped from a height, not eroded or embedded into the slope — there's a hard seam where each rock meets the hillside with no blending soil, moss transition, or dug material to explain the join. The hill silhouette itself is a plain, unbroken dome: no burrow mounds, no spoil-heaps, no secondary bumps that would read as "several large diggers made this," so at a glance it is indistinguishable from ordinary terrain. Colour-focus is a real problem: the single red-leaved tree left-of-center is the most saturated, highest-contrast object in the frame, and it is not the landmark — the bank behind it is supposed to be the landmark and it's the dullest, lowest-saturation shape in the shot. A viewer's eye goes to the tree, not the mound. The foreground grass/flower-bed reads fine (decent scatter variety), but everything above knee height is flat single-tone khaki-green with no texture variation to suggest packed dirt versus grass versus rock.

**02-knoll-from-outside.** Same hill, viewed along its length, and it confirms the silhouette problem: it's a long, low, gently undulating ridge with a completely regular skyline — no single peak, no obvious "this is the mound to walk toward." At 30% size this reads as background scenery, not a destination. The rock clusters at the base look like loose boulders parked in a line rather than material a creature displaced digging a den; nothing shows directional throw of earth (a dig mound has an entrance-facing spoil pile, angled slopes, disturbed color/vegetation gradient near the hole). Again a dead tree and a red tree book-end the ridge and are the highest-contrast items in frame — the landmark-that-should-be-a-landmark is upstaged by set-dressing trees. Grass color is a slightly washed, low-saturation green-grey that reads more swampy than meadow-vibrant next to the keyart's saturated greens.

**03-mouth.** This is the frame that should sell "dug burrow entrance" and it partially works and partially doesn't. The opening shape (two pointed brow-like arcs meeting at a peak) is distinctive and does look organic/gnawed rather than architectural, which is right. But: (1) the entrance is full black — there's no ambient fill or rim light suggesting depth or letting the eye read it as a hole rather than a flat silhouette cutout pasted onto the rock face; (2) the surrounding rock geometry is the same blocky brown boulders from frames 01/02, arranged in a rough ring, and they don't read as bank material parted by digging — it looks like a cave built from stacked rocks, not a bore into an earthen bank; (3) scale is ambiguous with no figure or prop at the threshold to size it against — going by the stated 3.4 m doorway the opening looks plausible width-wise, but there's nothing in this frame to confirm it; (4) the loose dead branches poking up above the entrance read as random clutter rather than intentional burrow dressing (a chewed root, exposed root mass hanging into the hole would sell "dug into a bank" better than sticks silhouetted against sky); (5) the same red tree crowds the right edge again, competing with the entrance for attention a third time in three frames.

**Overall exterior verdict:** the mouth is closer to working than the bank is. The bank itself does not read as a landmark mound — it reads as generic hillside terrain with rocks glued on, and the recurring red tree (not the bank) is what actually pulls focus in all three exterior frames, which is exactly backwards for a location that's supposed to be the visual draw pulling the player across the meadow.

## Interior (04–07)

**04-hall-dressing.** A believable low-key storage nook — crate, barrel, sack, a dark shape that might be a boulder or rubble, plus a foreground rock wall cutting the frame. It's static and cluttered-in-a-good-way rather than empty, which works. The rock-wall texture close to camera is muddy and low-contrast (looks like a stretched, blurry rock material rather than a modeled or well-lit surface) and there's an odd thin dark spider-like silhouette against the back wall on the right that isn't legible as anything (asset problem or debris — either way it reads as visual noise, not intentional dressing).

**05-hall-from-the-doorway.** The mushroom cluster is a nice, deliberate foreground landmark inside the dungeon — good silhouette variety against the tunnel walls, and it gives scale/depth to the corridor. Beyond it, a doorway frames the guardian creature down the hall, which is a reasonable teaser composition. The tunnel walls and ceiling beams have decent rock texture and directional light spilling from the den ahead, so this frame reads the best of the interior set for "authored space with depth."

**06-den-and-guardian.** The guardian is well-lit and centrally placed with a warm key light that separates it from the dark room — good staging for a boss reveal. Scale: measured against the ~3.4 m doorway visible to its left and the room's proportions, the creature reads as roughly waist/chest-height on a human, maybe 1.2–1.5 m at the shoulder in a crouched pose — that is not obviously threatening for a den guardian; it looks more like a large badger you'd fight early-game than a boss that intimidates. Its rounded, curled-up posture (like it's resting/defensive rather than aggressive) undercuts the threat read further — nothing in the pose, stance, or scale communicates "this thing can hurt you." The room itself (visible support beams, side doorway, floor debris) is one of the better-dressed interior spaces in the set.

**07-den-dressing.** Loot dressing (sack, crate, small mushroom cluster) in a very dark corner — so dark that the cross-beam wall texture and a distant dead-branch/root silhouette in the upper-left are barely legible. This frame is underlit to the point of being hard to read at all, let alone at 30% size; if there's any additional creature/environment detail beyond the loot props, it is currently invisible.

## Specific addressable defects (by frame)

- **01, 02**: hill silhouette is a single smooth dome/ridge with no bumps, mounds, or spoil-heaps that would read as "dug by large creatures" — needs an irregular, mounded skyline distinct from ordinary terrain.
- **01, 02, 03**: the red-leaved tree is consistently the highest-saturation, highest-contrast object in every exterior frame, competing with (and beating) the bank/mouth for attention in all three shots. Either desaturate/recolor it or move it out of the primary sightline to the entrance.
- **01, 02**: rock outcrops sit on top of the hill with a hard, unblended seam — no soil/moss transition suggesting they're embedded, not placed.
- **02**: boulder cluster at the base reads as parked/loose rather than displaced dig-spoil; no directional throw of earth toward or away from an entrance.
- **03**: entrance interior is pure black with no depth cue, no rim light, no visible threshold detail (root mass, worn dirt lip) that would sell "burrowed," and no figure/prop at the opening to confirm the stated 3.4 m scale.
- **03**: dead branches above the entrance read as random clutter, not burrow dressing.
- **04**: unidentified thin dark silhouette against the back wall reads as noise, not a prop; foreground rock-wall texture is muddy/low-contrast at close range.
- **06**: guardian's curled, low posture and modest scale relative to the doorway undercut the "boss" read — it looks defensive/small rather than dangerous.
- **07**: frame is under-lit to the point most of the geometry and dressing is unreadable, even accounting for dungeon mood.

## Verdict

**1. The three things that most separate these frames from the references, ranked:**

1. **No landmark silhouette.** Palworld's plateau/field shots (`palworld-04`, `palworld-03`) and the keyart's standing stone/mountain panels all give the eye one unmistakable large shape to read as "go there." The Warrens' bank (01, 02) is a smooth, low, undifferentiated ridge — the opposite of a landmark — and a decorative tree keeps stealing the frame's contrast instead.
2. **Colour focus is inverted.** The keyart and Palworld shots reserve their highest saturation for what matters — the pal, the boss, the landmark tower — and keep environment mid-tones subdued around it. Here the reddest, most eye-catching object in three consecutive exterior frames is a background tree, not the dungeon entrance the player is meant to walk toward.
3. **The guardian doesn't threaten.** Palworld's field-boss frame (`palworld-03`) makes its boss huge, backlit, and mid-attack with effects selling danger even in a still. Frame 06 gives a warmly-lit but small, curled, resting-looking creature — the staging (light, framing) is good, but the creature's scale and pose read as prey, not guardian.

**2. The two bar questions:**

**A. Do these frames read as belonging to the world in the keyart?** No. The keyart's meadow exteriors are saturated, high-value-range, with clear landmark silhouettes (the tower, the standing stone, the mountain) against a bright sky. These exterior frames have a comparatively flat, low-saturation hill under a nice sky, with a tree doing more visual work than the terrain feature that's supposed to be the destination. The palette isn't wrong (it's a plausible meadow green/khaki), but the landmark language the keyart establishes — one bold, legible shape you'd walk toward — is absent.

**B. Shown beside the Palworld shots, would someone say these are trying to be the same kind of game?** No, mainly on the exterior. Palworld's outdoor shots have dense, varied foliage, strong landmark massing, and readable creature scale even in an action still; these exterior frames have sparse mid-ground dressing, a soft/uneventful hill, and nothing mid-frame that says "adventure destination." The interior frames (04–07) come closer — the mushroom-cluster composition in 05 and the lit-guardian staging in 06 are the kind of authored beats Palworld also uses — but the exterior, which is the first and most important impression, does not clear the bar.

**What's fixable by changing the scene (density/palette/lighting/composition/scatter), and what needs new art:**

- *Fixable in-scene*: hill silhouette (reshape/add mounds via terrain sculpting, no new asset needed), tree placement/saturation (move or recolor the existing red tree asset, or add a duller variant near the entrance and save the vivid one for elsewhere), entrance interior lighting (add a warm interior light bleed near the threshold, common lighting-only fix), frame 07's overall exposure, and the unidentified dark silhouette in frame 04 (likely a placement/lighting fix or removing a stray decal).
- *Needs new art or a different asset*: dig-spoil / disturbed-earth geometry or texture at the bank base and entrance lip (there is no "displaced dirt" material in evidence — this needs a texture/mesh, not just rearranging existing rocks); a guardian pose or scale change if the intent is genuine boss threat (a curled/resting rig reads as passive regardless of lighting — that's a pose/rig or creature-selection decision, not a lighting fix).
